**Authors:** T Ramirez, **S Sabouri**, K Shenoy, A Jiang, M Martinez, Z Chavez, S Chattopadhyay, J May, M Guo
**Venue:** SGO 2027 Winter Meeting (accepted abstract, poster presentation)

## Summary
A pilot of an HPV vaccine counseling chatbot in a safety-net gynecologic oncology clinic, used by
patients before their visit. After using it, patients knew more about HPV and held fewer vaccine
misconceptions. Providers reported less strain during counseling, and fewer of their patients
arrived with general vaccine distrust or a lack of information. The time spent on counseling did
not change; the chatbot appears to have changed the quality of the session rather than its length.

## Participants
The pilot included 18 patients, nearly all Hispanic or Latino women aged 29 to 45 with a
gynecologic cancer or precancer diagnosis, most of them recent. They had very little prior
exposure to the HPV vaccine: only 3 had ever discussed it with a provider, and 16 of 18 did not
know anyone who had received it.

Every participant used a smartphone and many had tried AI tools, but they approached AI for health
with caution. Most expected it to misread everyday language, half said explaining their concerns
would be harder than with a doctor, and most would defer to their physician if the two disagreed.

## Findings: Patients
- **Knowledge.** HPV knowledge improved significantly: the average score rose from 2.5 to 3.5 out
  of 6 (p = .005), and "don't know" answers dropped from 52 to 35.
- **Misconceptions.** Two vaccine myths declined significantly: that the vaccine is only for girls
  and women (p = .010), and "I am not personally at risk" (p = .008). Overall, 13 of 18
  participants reduced their misconceptions (p = .006).
- **Perception of the chatbot.** Mean satisfaction was 4.23 out of 5, with the chatbot rated
  especially clear and helpful. Usability was close to average (SUS = 66). Trust was more modest,
  averaging 3.46: participants generally saw its information as accurate and reliable, but only
  half trusted it enough to follow its guidance, and half worried about the privacy of their
  information.
- **Intent.** After the visit, 8 of 18 said they would probably or definitely get the vaccine,
  8 were unsure, and 2 leaned no.

## Findings: Providers
- **Strain.** The share of providers who felt rushed because counseling ran over dropped from 42%
  to 7% (p < .001), and the share who felt they knew what should be done but could not achieve it
  fell from 48% to 13% (p = .005). Guilt when counseling failed and frustration over systemic
  barriers also declined significantly.
- **Patient concerns.** Providers saw fewer concerns about general vaccine distrust (69% to 13%)
  and lack of information (60% to 13%), suggesting the chatbot handled much of the basic education
  before the visit. Safety and side effects remained the most common concern (47%) — the
  misconception the chatbot could not resolve.
- **Perceived effectiveness.** The share rating their counseling "very" or "extremely" effective
  rose from 33% to 47% for patient understanding and from 26% to 40% for uptake, though post-visit
  ratings were more spread out.
- **Time.** The share of the visit spent on vaccine counseling stayed the same, under 10% for most
  providers (p = .64).
